## Format of the round

**Where this round sits.** Tesla's supply-chain loop, as described by candidates, is roughly:
recruiter screen → interview with a GSM / hiring manager on the team → panel with a ~30-minute
candidate-chosen presentation, followed by 1:1s with each panellist. One Blind poster preparing for
the Tesla Global Supply Manager final round in Dec 2021 was told "they will be asking me to present a
case in front of a panel"; the reply, from someone who had done it, was "you will decide what you
want to present. Choose something where you played a major role. Highlight your part in the entire
project. Then be prepared to answer their questions."

A separate 2024 Blind post from a candidate in a Tesla GSM process reports that the **first interview
round was "a live excel assessment round with hiring manager"** — an Excel-based sourcing assessment.
The poster was asking whether anyone had seen it, so the format is confirmed as scheduled but not as
experienced; no comment in the thread described what was in it.
[reported, 1 source — anecdote, and unconfirmed]
https://www.teamblind.com/post/tesla-gsm-interview-ta3hofyp

**This matters for Tuesday.** There is a real, if thinly evidenced, possibility that a Tesla supply

## What is actually graded

### Tesla's own words about the job (recovered from JD mirrors, not from tesla.com)

Tesla describes the Supply Chain Organization as "supporting the development, implementation and
management of strategies to provide Tesla with optimal supply assurance, pricing, flexibility,
quality and delivery performance," with the Global Supply Manager building relationships with
"engineering teams and key suppliers to assure cost, quality, and delivery targets are met," and
working in "a fast moving, dynamic environment where flexibility and adaptability are key attributes
needed for success."
[published — Tesla JD text, fetched via a job-board mirror because tesla.com 403s]
https://www.wayup.com/i-Manufacturing-j-Tesla-Motors-531471381173647/

A current Tesla Supply Chain Manager req lists as a hard requirement: "Advanced proficiency in
should-cost modeling, cost breakdown analysis, and total cost of ownership frameworks," plus a
"proven track record leading complex commercial negotiations and structuring supplier agreements at
scale," and building "should-cost models to perform rigorous cost analysis of supplier proposals."
The same req asks for a degree "together with evidence of exceptional ability" — the
Evidence-of-Excellence idea written into the requirements themselves.
[published — Tesla JD text via mirror]
https://simplify.jobs/p/0207003c-96b2-47bd-b7f9-6be541ff31e4/Capital-Expenditure-Supply-Chain-Manager

**So the five things the JD itself puts weight on, and therefore the five things to expect to be
drilled on:** (1) a bottom-up should-cost model you built from an understanding of the manufacturing
process; (2) a negotiation you personally ran — "Excellent negotiator" is called out explicitly in
this req; (3) supplier qualification / RFI–RFQ mechanics; (4) how you operate with Engineering in
design reviews and change control; (5) a category business plan that ties market conditions and
technology roadmap to a sourcing strategy. [inference — rests on the JD text above plus the standard
practice that a hiring-manager round tests the JD's stated requirements]

### First-principles cost thinking

This is the single most Tesla-specific thing in the round, and it has a published origin. Musk's
articulation, from a 2012 interview: "What are the material constituents of the batteries? What is
the spot market value of the material constituents?" — he then priced the constituents at London
Metal Exchange spot, got ~$80/kWh against a prevailing pack price of ~$600/kWh, and concluded the gap
was a manufacturing problem, not a materials problem.
[published]
https://www.startuparchive.org/p/elon-musk-explains-first-principles-thinking-and-uses-it-to-predict-80-decline-in-battery-prices

That is exactly the shape of a should-cost model, and it is the shape the hiring manager will be
listening for: start from mass × material price, add process time × machine rate, add tooling
amortised over volume, add scrap, add SG&A and margin — then ask why the quote differs.
[inference — connects Musk's published reasoning to the standard should-cost element structure
below]

Standard should-cost element structure, for reference:
Should-Cost = Direct Materials + Labour + Manufacturing Overhead + Process (machining/forming/
casting/moulding/QA) + Logistics + Tooling & NRE + Margin (typically 5–20%). Data sources: RFQ
responses, ERP history, regional wage data, published indices. Named pitfalls: unverified inputs,
lumping costs into broad buckets instead of granular elements, and letting the model go stale as
markets move.
[published]
https://precoro.com/blog/should-cost-model-how-to-build-calculate-and-negotiate/

### Cost-down as an actual, stated Tesla behaviour

Tesla is on record as a company that reopens signed deals. In July 2018 it asked suppliers to return
"a meaningful amount of money" of payments made since 2016. Tesla's own statement: "Negotiation is a
standard part of the procurement process, and now that we're in a stronger position with Model 3
production ramping, it is a good time to improve our competitive advantage in this area." Tesla
clarified it "asked fewer than 10 suppliers for a reduction in total capex project spend for
long-term projects that began in 2016 but are still not complete."
[published — Tesla's own statement, quoted; 2018, so old, but it is the clearest published statement
of Tesla's posture toward suppliers I could find]
https://electrek.co/2018/07/23/tesla-suppliers-reduce-cost-become-profitable/

This is useful two ways: it tells you the expected aggression level, and it tells you the hiring
manager will not be shocked by a hard-nosed negotiation story. It also means a candidate who frames
supplier relationships purely as partnership-preservation will read as soft for this house.
[inference]

### Communication style Tesla explicitly rewards

Musk's standing internal rule, first issued at SpaceX in May 2010 and reissued to Tesla staff in the
April 2018 productivity email: acronyms and jargon are an impediment. "Excessive use of made up
acronyms is a significant impediment to communication and keeping communication good as we grow is
incredibly important." The 2018 Tesla version added: don't use acronyms or nonsense words for
objects, software or processes at Tesla — "anything that requires an explanation inhibits
communication" — and let information flow directly rather than up and down a chain of command.
[published for the SpaceX 2010 email, which I fetched in full; reported for the 2018 Tesla email,
whose text I saw only in search-engine summaries after cnbc.com and inc.com both 403'd]
https://gist.github.com/klaaspieter/12cd68f54bb71a3940eae5cdd4ea1764

Practical read: a supply-chain candidate who narrates in procurement acronyms — PPV, TCO, LTA, SCAR,
APQP, VA/VE, MOQ, EAU — without unpacking them is working against a stated cultural preference.
Say the thing. [inference]

## Question archetypes

Six shapes. Surface wording varies; what each is testing does not.

**1. "Walk me through a should-cost model you built."**
The signature question of this round. Testing whether you can go from a physical part to a number
without a supplier's help: material mass and grade, process route and cycle time, machine rate,
tooling cost and the volume it amortises over, scrap, freight, overhead, margin. And then whether you
can name your own model's weakest assumption. A candidate report specifically cites being asked about
should-cost modelling in a Tesla GSM interview.
[reported, 1 source — and I could not fetch it; see Gaps. Corroborated indirectly by the JD, which
makes should-cost modelling a hard requirement.]

**2. The negotiation war story.**
"Excellent negotiator" is in the req verbatim. Testing: did you have a walk-away and a BATNA, or did
you split the difference? What leverage did you actually create — dual-source, volume commitment,
tooling ownership, index-linked pricing, extended payment terms — versus what you were handed?
Reported generic-role variants: "Describe a challenging negotiation with a supplier," "Can you give
an example of a successful negotiation you have had with a supplier?"
[published for the JD requirement; reported, 2 sources for the archetype]
https://interviewprep.org/commodity-manager-interview-questions/

**3. Supplier crisis / capacity shock / de-risking.**
A reported Tesla-specific version: "What do you do if you are told to double your forecast overnight
and the supplier says they do not have capacity to support that sudden demand?" Testing: whether you
go to the constraint (which operation is the bottleneck — the braze furnace, the mould, the press?)
rather than to the relationship, and whether you have actually stood up a second source under time
pressure. Generic variants: "Describe a time when you had to manage a supplier crisis."
[reported — the Tesla-specific wording appeared in a search-engine summary of a Glassdoor page that
returned 403 to the fetcher, so I am reporting it as unverified; the generic archetype is confirmed
in a fetched source]
https://interviewprep.org/commodity-manager-interview-questions/

**4. Cross-functional conflict with Engineering.**
A reported Tesla GSM question: how you would deal with issues working cross-functionally with SIEs
and DEs — Supplier Industrialization Engineers and Design Engineers. This is not abstract at Tesla:
the SIE job description has SIEs guiding Design Engineers on DFM against supplier fabrication limits,
running APQP/PPAP with tier 2s, issuing SCARs, and coordinating NPI, line trials, design changes,
sourcing changes and supplier change requests. Supply chain sits inside that triangle. Testing:
whether you can hold a cost position against an engineer who wants a tighter tolerance, and whether
you understand where your authority ends.
[reported for the interview question — from a search-engine summary of an unfetchable Glassdoor page;
published for the SIE/DE working model, fetched]
https://tesla.veteran-hiring.com/job-detail/supplier-industrialization-engineer-pcb-224502

**5. Cost-reduction ownership.**
Testing whether you drove a number down and can attribute it. Expect the follow-up to be about
mechanism, not magnitude: resourcing, VA/VE, design change, tooling move, index renegotiation,
consolidation. Tesla req language for a sibling role names "'Lean' initiatives" and "sourcing
efficiencies via benchmarking, VA/VE, competitive quotes."
[reported — the "Lean" / VA/VE / benchmarking phrasing was seen only in a search-engine summary of a
Tesla Manufacturing-CapEx req whose page 404'd; the fetched Tesla JD (source #6) does confirm
cost-optimisation and TCO framing]

**6. "Why Tesla, why this team, why now."**
Testing mission alignment with a technical hook, and whether you know what Tesla's thermal
architecture actually is. Reported standard wording: "Why Tesla? Why are you interested in joining
this team specifically?"
[reported, 2 sources]
https://customcareer.miami.edu/blog/2026/05/14/get-a-job-at-tesla-interview-process-and-top-questions/

Two more that appear in Tesla loops generally and could land here:
"Tell me about a time you raised the bar." · "Describe delivering something in half the planned
timeline." [reported, 1 source, same URL as above]

## Thermal systems: the technical floor the hiring manager can drill to

This section exists because the round is category-specific. A Tesla thermal commodity manager who
cannot name the parts and how they are made cannot build a should-cost model, and the hiring manager
knows it. The following is well evidenced.

**The architecture.** Tesla's Model Y-generation thermal system consolidates what conventional OEMs
split across several cooling systems into one integrated assembly, roughly briefcase-sized:

- **Octovalve** — a rotary valve with eight ports and five internal positions, driven by a motorised
  reduction gearbox through ~270° rotations; described elsewhere as two stacked 4-way valves with a
  stepper motor turning a central disc. Directs glycol coolant between cabin, HV battery, drive
  units and electronics.
- **Coolant (water-glycol) manifold** — multi-port **moulded nylon/polymer**.
- **Refrigerant "super-manifold"** — **aluminium alloy, semi-solid forged**, containing passages,
  valving and an integrated accumulator; produced using **friction-stir welding** and **electrical
  discharge machining**.
- **Heat pump loop** — compressor, chiller, water-cooled condenser, evaporator, accumulator,
  thermal expansion valves; refrigerant **R-1234yf**.
- **Coolant loop** — ethylene-glycol (HOAT), **two electric water pumps**, a **single air-to-coolant
  radiator with active shutters**, cold plates bonded to cells.
- **Cabin HVAC** with integrated fan; **electric (PTC-type) heater**.

Manufacturing processes named in the teardown: **brazing** (plate to forging), **extrusion** (the
chiller), **polymer moulding** (the coolant manifold), plus the forging/FSW/EDM above. The consolidation
claim: "three main parts instead of the hundreds — including hoses, fasteners, clips and interfaces"
used conventionally. Sandy Munro called it "totally different" from conventional OEM designs and
compared it to a jet engine fuel system. Reported range benefit of the heat pump + octovalve
architecture: ~10% efficiency improvement on Model Y.
[published — Munro & Associates teardown analysis, two independent write-ups]
https://www.emobility-engineering.com/tesla-octovalve/ (May 2021) ·
https://vasa.org.au/inside-teslas-octovalve/ (17 Jun 2020)

**What a cost model for these parts looks like.** For the brazed-aluminium family (radiators, chiller,
condenser, cold plates), the cost drivers are material mass at aluminium price, factory time, braze
route (vacuum-brazed plate-fin is the most expensive; mechanically assembled the cheapest but less
efficient), and tolerance — tighter plumbing tolerances mean more expensive tooling plus more labour
and inspection time. For the moulded manifold, the standard breakdown is tool steel and standard
parts at 15–30% of tool investment, processing 20–30%, then design, trials, packaging, freight and
margin, with the tool amortised over expected annual usage.
[reported, 2 sources — I could not fetch the Boyd/Eaton cost-driver page (timeout twice) so the
brazing/tolerance detail comes from search-engine summaries of it; the injection-mould breakdown
likewise came from a search summary. Treat the percentages as indicative, not citable.]

**Supply base and current market conditions — the "category business plan" material.**
Tier 1s in EV thermal: Denso (est. 18–22% share), Valeo, MAHLE, Hanon Systems, BorgWarner, Modine,
Dana, with Sanhua emerging from China. The clear direction of travel is module-level integration —
Hanon in 2026 shipped a 16 kg module combining an eCompressor, electronic expansion valve block,
water-cooled condenser, internal heat exchanger, chiller, A/C lines and sensors as one assembly.
Named current constraint: "Electric compressor production is tight relative to the surge in heat pump
adoption" across EVs, grid storage and buildings simultaneously — three markets competing for the
same components.
[published for the supply-chain structure and the compressor constraint]
https://www.electronsx.com/thermal-system-supply-chain.html

**The cost headwind you should be able to name.** Aluminium is the dominant material in this
commodity, and US Section 232 has been restructured. From 6 April 2026 the single 50% metals tariff
was replaced by a tiered system: 50% on articles wholly steel/aluminium/copper, 25% on downstream
manufactured articles, a 15% tier for further-downstream goods through 2027 — and, critically, **the
duty is now assessed on full customs value rather than metal content**, which raises effective rates
on low-metal-content assemblies. Autos and auto parts carry 25%; USMCA-qualifying parts are exempt.
Section 232 is described as "largely unavoidable by switching countries," which pushes mitigation
toward domestic sourcing, USMCA preference, material substitution or foreign trade zones.
[published, 2 sources]
https://globaltradealert.org/blog/s232-metals-restructuring-april-2026 (4 Apr 2026) ·
https://www.tariffstool.com/guides/section-232-tariffs-steel-aluminum-guide

**The business pressure the hiring manager is under.** Tesla Q2 2026: record revenue $28.24B (+26%
YoY), but operating income down 57% to $398M, operating margin 1.4%, gross margin 16.8%, capex $5.79B
(+142%), free cash flow −$1.09B — the first negative quarter since early 2024. Tesla's own framing is
that it is "in its largest and most exciting period of investment."
[published — reported from Tesla's Q2 2026 shareholder deck; I could not fetch the SEC-hosted deck
itself (403), so this is a secondary account of a primary document]
https://evwire.com/p/tesla-q2-2026-earnings-results (22 Jul 2026)

That is the context for a commodity manager hire: margin is compressed, capex is enormous, and the
cost line is where the pressure lands. A candidate who has read this and can say so plainly is
answering "why now" with evidence. [inference]

## What separates a strong answer from a weak one

**Strong: a number with a mechanism attached.** "We took $4.10 out of the assembly — $2.60 by moving
the braze from a vacuum furnace to CAB after the tolerance was opened from ±0.05 to ±0.15, $1.50 by
re-amortising the tool over the second programme." Weak: "I delivered significant cost savings across
my commodity." The Tesla-specific corroboration is the Evidence-of-Excellence guidance, whose strong
example is a fully quantified single result and whose named-weak examples are "passionate,"
"detail-oriented," "team player."
[reported, 1 source, plus inference for the thermal-specific illustration]

**Strong: bottom-up, not benchmark.** Building the number from mass × price + cycle time × machine
rate + tooling ÷ volume is what the JD asks for ("generating bottom-up should-cost models from
understanding of manufacturing process") and what Musk's published battery reasoning models. Weak:
"we benchmarked against last year's PPV and targeted 5%." A year-on-year percentage is a target, not
a model, and this round can tell the difference.
[inference — rests on the JD language and the Musk quote, both cited above]

**Strong: naming your model's weakest assumption before you're asked.** The published pitfall list for
should-cost models leads with unverified inputs and over-aggregated cost buckets. Volunteering "my
machine rate was a regional average, which is the softest number in the model" is the move that
converts a spreadsheet into judgement.
[published for the pitfalls; inference for the interview application]
https://precoro.com/blog/should-cost-model-how-to-build-calculate-and-negotiate/

**Strong: "I" with the decision attached.** Weak: "we." The reported Tesla trap follow-up is "What was
your role specifically?" — and once it has been asked, the answer has already lost something.
[reported, 2 sources]

**Strong: the engineer conversation reconstructed, not summarised.** "The DE wanted a 0.1mm profile
tolerance on the cold-plate face. I asked the supplier what it cost to hold it and what the yield was
at 0.2mm, brought both numbers to the design review, and we changed the drawing." Weak: "I have
excellent cross-functional stakeholder management skills." Tesla's own SIE job description shows the
DFM-versus-design negotiation happening explicitly and continuously, so a candidate who describes it
in competency language rather than in specifics reads as someone who has watched it rather than done
it.
[published for the SIE/DE working model; inference for the strong/weak split]

**Strong: plain words.** Musk's standing instruction against acronyms and jargon is a published,
company-wide preference. Unpacking a term once and then using it costs three seconds and reads as
clarity; running a whole answer in procurement shorthand reads as someone hiding behind the shorthand.
[published for the instruction; inference for the interview consequence]

**Strong: a specific technical reason for wanting this team.** The reported failure mode is the
generic sustainable-energy answer. The reported success pattern is naming a specific Tesla
engineering choice. For this role that means the octovalve/heat-pump consolidation — one supplier
assembly replacing an OEM's several — and what that does to the sourcing problem.
[reported, 3 sources for the pattern; published for the technical substance]

**Weak, specifically for this round: the partnership-only negotiator.** Tesla has publicly reopened
signed supplier agreements and described doing so as "a standard part of the procurement process."
A candidate whose entire negotiation philosophy is relationship preservation is answering a different
company's question.
[published for Tesla's statement; inference for the consequence]

## Traps

**1. Preparing this as the behavioural round and finding a spreadsheet.**
One 2024 candidate report has the Tesla GSM first interview round being a live Excel-based sourcing
assessment with the hiring manager. Video on, over Teams, screen-share is one click. If a cost model
appears, the failure mode is going silent while you think. Narrate the build.
[reported, 1 source — anecdote, and even that poster had not yet sat it]

**2. Bringing a top-down cost story to a bottom-up question.**
The JD asks for models "generated from understanding of manufacturing process." The trap is that
year-on-year percentage savings sound like a good answer everywhere else in procurement and sound
like an evasion here. The tell is that you cannot answer "what's the machine rate you used?"
[inference — rests on the JD language, the Musk first-principles quote, and the published should-cost
element structure]

**3. Not knowing how the parts are made.**
"Technical and commercial understanding of tooling and manufacturing processes" is a stated
requirement. A thermal commodity spans brazing, stamping, extrusion, injection moulding, tube bending,
forging and FSW — and the cost of a Tesla thermal assembly is dominated by process route and tolerance
rather than by material alone. Saying "I'd work with the supplier to understand their cost structure"
is a request for the supplier to write your model for you, which is the opposite of the job.
[published for the process list and the tolerance/cost link; inference for the failure mode]

**4. Jargon and acronyms.**
A stated, company-wide Musk instruction, twice issued. Procurement is one of the most acronym-dense
functions there is; this is a real, and easily avoidable, own goal.
[published for the instruction; inference for the trap]

**5. "We."**
Reported rejection pattern with a named follow-up question. Every claim in this round should have a
verb you personally performed.
[reported, 2 sources]

**6. Treating the final round as the one that matters.**
The reported final round is a candidate-chosen presentation to a panel followed by 1:1s. But the
hiring manager conversation is where the story you will present gets chosen and where levelling
starts. Deciding in this round what your presentation case will be — and saying so — converts a
screening call into a working conversation.
[reported, 1 source for the panel format; inference for the implication]

**7. Not naming the tariff and market position.**
The req asks for category business plans covering "market conditions" and "technology roadmaps."
Section 232 restructured on 6 April 2026, moved to full-customs-value assessment, and aluminium is
this commodity's dominant material. A candidate for an aluminium-heavy category who has not formed a
view on it has skipped the part of the job the req names.
[published for the tariff facts; inference for the interview consequence]

## Timing

**Commit: 120 seconds per answer, with a 90-second green zone.**

Reasoning, and the evidence under it:

- The round is reported at 30–45 minutes, run by one person, covering background, technical depth,
  team fit and 90-day expectations. At 45 minutes with a two-way conversation, that is roughly 12–18
  exchanges — which puts the natural answer length at around two minutes before follow-ups.
  [reported, 4 sources for the 30–45 minutes; inference for the arithmetic]
- Published guidance on answer length: 30 seconds to 4 minutes depending on question type, with
  simple questions at 30 seconds to 2 minutes and complex behavioural questions at 2 to 4 minutes.
  [published] https://www.indeed.com/career-advice/interviewing/how-long-should-interview-answers-be
- Wider STAR consensus, which I saw in multiple search summaries but could fetch only one member of:
  90 seconds to 2 minutes, with roughly 20% situation / 10% task / 50% action / 20% result.
  [reported — one fetched source plus search-engine summaries; the "interviewers disengage after two
  minutes" claim that circulates with this guidance is not attributed to any study I could verify,
  and I am not relying on it]

**How to use the number.** 90 seconds is the floor for a substantive answer: enough for the situation
in one sentence, what you did in six, and the number at the end. 120 seconds is the target. Past 150
seconds in this round you are almost certainly narrating context that the hiring manager would rather
spend on the drill-down — and in a Tesla round the drill-down is where the assessment actually
happens, so leaving room for it is not a courtesy, it is strategy.

**Two exceptions.** A should-cost walkthrough may legitimately run to 150–180 seconds because you are
narrating a build with steps. A live Excel exercise is not timed by this rule at all; there, the rule
is continuous narration rather than length. [inference]

## Contradictions and gaps

**Contradictions in the sources**

1. *Where the hiring manager round sits.* Two guides place the hiring-manager call as stage 2–3,
   early, before technical screens. Two others fold the hiring-manager conversation into the final
   onsite panel. The user's own situation — recruiter screen passed, HM next, presentation panel
   after — matches the "early" version, so I have weighted that. But it means the "hiring manager as
   final fit conversation" framing in some guides does not apply here.
2. *Loop length.* "3 interviews plus recruiter talks, ~2 months" (supply-chain-specific, from a search
   summary of an unfetchable page) versus "3–5 weeks" and "median 33 days" (general Tesla, fetched).
   Both may be true — supply-chain reqs may simply move slower than the company median — but they are
   not reconciled by any source.
3. *Whether there is a live assessment in the HM round.* One 2024 report says yes (Excel sourcing
   assessment with the hiring manager). No other source mentions it. It is a single anecdote from
   someone who had not yet sat it. I have flagged it as a possibility to prepare for, not a fact.
4. *Number of onsite rounds.* Ranges from "3 interviews total" to "3–5 back-to-back 45–60 minute
   rounds" to "5–6 loop interviews, all five days in office" across sources, none of them
   supply-chain-specific with confidence.

**What I could not establish**

- **Anything Tesla itself says about how it interviews.** Every `tesla.com/careers` URL I tried
  (US, CA, GB, JO locales, plus the supply-chain team page and individual job pages) returned HTTP
  403. There is no Tesla equivalent of Amazon's published interview-prep pages that I could reach.
- **The Glassdoor corpus.** `glassdoor.com` returned 403 on every attempt, as did `nodeflair.com`.
  These hold the 12–14 reported Tesla Global Supply Manager interview reviews. Several specific
  claims in this dossier — the should-cost question, the SIE/DE cross-functional question, the
  "double your forecast overnight" capacity question, the "commodity understanding / contract /
  supply planning / costing / shortage management / relationship building" panel topic list, the
  "constant ambiguity with no resources" probe — **came to me only as search-engine summaries of
  pages I could not open.** I have marked each one at the point of use. They are plausible and
  mutually consistent, but none is a verified quotation and Phase 2 should not present any of them
  as a candidate quote.
- **Whether a scorecard or named competency list exists.** No evidence either way. The single source
  describing a "hiring committee" and an "Elon approval" layer is an interview-prep blog with no
  attribution.
- **How the round opens.** Not established by any source. The Evidence-of-Excellence field is a
  reasonable guess at the opening prompt, but it is a guess.
- **Anything specific to req 277610.** The exact posting was not retrievable; nothing I found names
  "Supply Chain Manager, Thermal Systems" with a description. The JD language quoted here is from
  sibling Tesla supply-chain reqs (CapEx, Battery Thermals, Solar, Raw Materials) recovered via
  mirrors. The user-supplied JD summary is more authoritative for this specific req than anything I
  found.
- **Verified cost figures for Tesla thermal parts.** No teardown source I reached publishes a bill of
  materials with prices for the octovalve or the manifolds. The cost-driver material here is generic
  to brazed-aluminium heat exchangers and moulded parts, not Tesla-specific.
- **Any interviewer-side debrief writing.** Nothing equivalent to the Amazon debrief write-ups exists
  in public for Tesla supply chain, as far as I could find.
- **Whether the Teams/video-on detail changes anything.** No source addresses Tesla's remote
  interview conduct specifically. Everything about the round would be the same in a room.

**Age of evidence.** The two Blind GSM threads are from Dec 2021 and Feb 2024; the Tesla supplier
cost-down statement is 2018; the octovalve teardowns are 2020–2021; the interview guides are dated
2026 but of unverifiable provenance; the tariff and Tesla-financials material is 2026 and solid. The
oldest material — Musk on first principles (2012) and acronyms (2010/2018) — is quoted because it is
still the stated operating philosophy, not because it is current news.
